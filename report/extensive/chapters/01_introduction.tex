\chapter{Introduction}\label{sec:introduction}

This report documents the design, analysis and verification of a printed airframe for a 3.5-inch quadcopter. The components are fixed: motors, propellers, flight controller, receiver, battery and GPS module were selected first, and the frame is the only part left open. It is printed in fused filament on a Bambu Lab P1S and must carry every load the components produce, keep its first elastic mode clear of the rotor excitations and fit every component with a defined assembly sequence.

The report is written as a complete record. Every number is either read from a data sheet, measured on a reference build, or derived in the text from the physics that produces it. The theory chapters build each result from its basic definition upwards, with a worked example in the project's own numbers, so that a reader can check every step without other sources.

\section{Goals}\label{sec:intro-goals}

The project has three goals.
\begin{enumerate}
\item A printed structure that costs less than a commercial carbon-fibre frame for the same components.
\item An honest measure of what fused-filament printing can achieve for a quadcopter airframe: where it matches carbon plates, where it falls short, and why.
\item A complete practical case of design, analysis and verification: requirements from first principles, a material chosen by a merit index, a topology-optimised structure, and checks on the geometry that is actually printed.
\end{enumerate}

The FlyFishRC Volador VX3.5~\citep{volador}, a frame of T700 carbon plates sold for \SI{37.91}{EUR}, serves as the commercial reference frame. It is a benchmark for mass, stiffness and cost. It is not a target the printed frame is expected to beat: a thermoplastic reinforced with short fibres has a much lower specific stiffness than continuous-fibre carbon plate, and the comparison exists to quantify that gap and the cost saving that comes with it.

\section{Method}\label{sec:intro-method}

The work follows one chain, in which each step hands a number to the next.
\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Requirements} (\cref{sec:requirements}): what the frame must do, stated with values and with the reason for each value.
\item \textbf{Sizing from first principles} (\cref{sec:sizing}): thrust, rotor speed, power and flight time from momentum theory and the manufacturer's bench data, and the cost of each gram and each millimetre of arm width in flight time.
\item \textbf{Loads} (\cref{sec:loads}): full-throttle forces and torques, load factors, landings and the rotor frequencies that excite the frame.
\item \textbf{Material} (\cref{sec:material}): a merit index for a stiffness-limited arm, gates for heat and printability, and design values checked against independent tests.
\item \textbf{Architecture and design space} (\cref{sec:architecture}): layout, the arm joint, and the regions the optimiser may fill, must keep and must leave empty.
\item \textbf{Topology optimisation} (\cref{sec:topopt_theory,sec:optimisation_model}): the theory, the model, the solver set-up and the production run.
\item \textbf{Verification} (\cref{sec:iterations,sec:verification}): the optimised density field thresholded into a printable body, checked again for every requirement, and the design-space changes that the checks forced.
\item \textbf{Strength and capacity} (\cref{sec:strength}): stresses on the printed geometry against the allowables, and the failure order between arm and joint.
\item \textbf{Integration} (\cref{sec:retainers}): the small parts that hold the receiver, the GPS and the antenna.
\end{enumerate}
\Cref{sec:results} compares the results with the requirements, states the shortfalls with their first-principles cause and sets out the next steps. \Cref{sec:logs} holds the decision log, the numbers and the assumptions; \cref{sec:workflow} describes the scripts and the workflow that produced them.

\section{Conventions}\label{sec:intro-conventions}

The model uses millimetres, newtons, tonnes and seconds, so stresses are in megapascals. The global axes are $x$ forward, $y$ left and $z$ up, with the origin at the frame centre and $z = 0$ on the top face of the arms, where the motors sit. The four corners are named by the signs of their coordinates: pp ($+x+y$), pm ($+x-y$), mp ($-x+y$) and mm ($-x-y$). Every part is printed with its layers in the $xy$ plane, so the build direction is the global $z$ axis throughout.

Masses are in grams. Bench data for propellers quote thrust in grams as well; this is gram-force, the weight of one gram, \SI{9.81e-3}{N}. Tables keep grams, and every physical formula uses newtons.
