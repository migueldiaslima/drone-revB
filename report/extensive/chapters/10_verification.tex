\chapter{Thresholded verification and print geometry}\label{sec:verification}

The optimiser ends with a density between 0 and 1 in every element. A printer can only deposit material or leave a gap, so the result has to be turned into a solid part, and the solid part has to be checked again: the frequencies and stiffnesses reported by the optimiser belong to a grey density field that cannot be built. This chapter describes the thresholded model that checks the buildable part against the requirements, the final check on the print geometry, and the steps from the density field to the print files with the checks made on them.

\section{Why a second check}\label{sec:verification-why}

A natural frequency depends on stiffness and moving mass. For a single degree of freedom,
\begin{equation}
f = \frac{1}{2\pi}\sqrt{\frac{k}{m}},
\end{equation}
and every elastic mode of the frame follows the same rule with the stiffness and mass that the mode shape engages.

The optimiser scales the stiffness and the mass of each element with its density through two interpolation functions. Measured from the optimiser's own output tables, an element at density 0.4 carries a stiffness factor of 0.1024 and a mass factor of 0.400. When the density field is cut at an iso-level below 0.4, that element becomes solid in the printed part:
\begin{align}
\text{stiffness:}\quad & \frac{1}{0.1024} = 9.8 \text{ times larger},\nonumber\\
\text{mass:}\quad & \frac{1}{0.400} = 2.5 \text{ times larger}.
\end{align}
An element below the cut disappears, and with it mostly mass: at density 0.2 the element carried 0.200 of its solid mass but only 0.0368 of its solid stiffness. Both effects tend to raise the ratio $k/m$ above what the optimiser credited, so the printed part is usually stiffer than predicted. The exception is a load path that rested on grey material below the cut: when it disappears, the part loses a support the optimiser counted on. The thresholded check exists to find such cases.

\section{The thresholded model}\label{sec:verification-model}

The thresholded model reuses the optimisation model and its mesh unchanged and replaces only the per-element material factors. For a cut at iso-level $c$:
\begin{itemize}
\item an element with density at or above $c$ gets stiffness factor 1 and mass factor 1 (solid PET-CF);
\item an element below $c$ gets stiffness factor \num{1e-6} and mass factor \num{1e-9} (air);
\item fixed elements are always solid.
\end{itemize}
Keeping the air elements in the mesh avoids remeshing and keeps every coupling, tie and support of the optimisation model in place. The air factors are small enough that the air carries no load and adds no mass: \num{1e-6} of the PET-CF stiffness is about \SI{0.005}{MPa}.

The optimisation responses are removed from the input, and the model runs as a plain analysis with the same frequency step, the same four load steps, the same three supports and the same eigenvalue shift. The acceptance criteria are those of the optimisation: modes 7 to 12 at or above \SI{417}{Hz}, the strain energy of every load case at or below its cap, and support reactions below \SI{1e-6}{N}.

Before the frequencies are read, a connectivity check looks for solid pieces that are not joined to the frame. It groups the solid elements into connected components through shared nodes, counting nodes closer than \SI{0.2}{mm} as connected so that the ties across the joint gaps and the lid seat count as connections. A fixed boss held only by elements below the cut appears as a separate small component. Such an island has no load path, and in the frequency step it vibrates as a nearly free body on its air elements: in the case found, six modes between 45 and \SI{200}{Hz} had their largest motion on one island, so the check also explains frequencies that would otherwise be puzzling. The two islands the check found, and the rules that removed them, are described in \cref{sec:iterations}.

\section{From density field to surface}\label{sec:verification-surface}

The density field is stored per element: a staircase. Cutting it element by element gives jagged faces and loose fragments, so the surface is built the way the optimiser's own smoothing does it:
\begin{enumerate}
\item Each corner node gets the mean density of the elements around it.
\item Inside each tetrahedron the density varies linearly between its four corner values, which gives a continuous field.
\item On each edge with one end above the iso-level $c$ and one below, the surface crosses at the fraction
\begin{equation}
t = \frac{c - \rho_a}{\rho_b - \rho_a}
\end{equation}
of the edge from node $a$; with $\rho_a = 0.8$, $\rho_b = 0.2$ and $c = 0.5$ the crossing lies halfway.
\item A cut tetrahedron has three or four crossed edges, which give a triangle or a quadrilateral split into two triangles. Neighbouring tetrahedra share their crossing points, so the triangles join into one closed skin.
\item Where material reaches the boundary of the design space, the boundary faces are added and trimmed at the crossing line.
\item Six iterations of Taubin smoothing remove the facet noise of the mesh. Taubin smoothing alternates a shrinking and an inflating step, so the volume changes only by tenths of a percent.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Choice of the iso-level}\label{sec:verification-iso}

A lower iso-level keeps more material, as a lower water level leaves more land dry. At both 0.4 and 0.3 every part is one closed piece, and the walls grow at the lower level. Iso 0.3 was chosen for wall margin at the lid front openings and under the motor pads, at a cost of \SI{3.57}{g} on the frame (\cref{tab:verification-masses}).

\section{Restoration of the exact features}\label{sec:verification-restoration}

The smooth surface rounds every edge, closes small holes partly and shifts flat faces by tenths of a millimetre. Bolt holes, seats, motor pads and the joint faces must keep their exact geometry, so the print part is rebuilt from the surface and the exact solids of the design files with three Boolean operations (\cref{fig:verification-restoration}). For the tub, with $S$ the smoothed surface, $D$ and $F$ the design and fixed regions, $K$ the keel design region and $A$, $B$ the two keels:
\begin{equation}
P = \bigl[\,S \cap \bigl((D \setminus K) \cup F\bigr)\bigr] \cup F \cup A \cup B.
\end{equation}
The intersection trims the surface to the design space, which removes everything that grew into holes, slots, seats and keep-outs. The union adds back the exact fixed solids and the keels. The lid and the arm follow the same formula without $K$, $A$ and $B$. The design files are meshed at \SI{0.3}{mm} for the Booleans; the meshed design and fixed volumes match the design files within \SI{0.02}{\percent}.

The order of the operations matters. Adding the fixed solids before trimming builds faces of the result on two coincident boundaries, and those become zero-thickness flaps: a check of the first restored tub found one of \SI{2.3}{mm^2} below a keel, and 550 back-to-back triangles over the whole part. Trimming first and adding the exact solids last removed them. The keel design region was also cleaned as a whole: a cleaning box slightly smaller than the region had left \SI{34.1}{mm^3} of skins \SIrange{0.15}{0.2}{mm} thick on its faces. After the Booleans, the mesh is cleaned once more (vertices welded within \SI{1e-5}{mm}, degenerate and back-to-back triangles removed) and loose pieces up to \SI{1}{mm^3} are dropped and listed: three in the tub (0.213, 0.188 and \SI{0.017}{mm^3}, inside the tub) and one of \SI{0.003}{mm^3} in the lid.

Wall thickness is measured in two ways, because each is biased in a different direction.
\begin{itemize}
\item The sphere method gives each point of the solid the diameter of the largest ball that fits inside the part and contains the point. It is evaluated on a voxel grid (\SI{0.1}{mm} for the lid and the arm, \SI{0.2}{mm} for the tub) by morphological opening. At a sharp convex edge no ball reaches the corner, so every sharp edge counts as thin; the restored parts carry many sharp edges from the exact features.
\item The ray method follows the inward normal from each surface triangle to the opposite surface. It measures the wall in front of each face, so edges count much less, but a Boolean film of a few hundredths of a millimetre counts as a thin wall.
\end{itemize}
